I spent the last five months building a meditation app for the Vision Pro. The thesis show was last Thursday. I am still recovering, and still thinking about one thing: the rectangle was never the destination.

For thirty years, every interface I have designed has fit inside a frame. Phone, tablet, laptop, monitor, projector. The frame is so old we forgot it was a constraint. We argued about pixel density and grid systems and what color the buttons should be, and never once asked whether the room itself could be the layout.

The Vision Pro is not a perfect device. The weight is wrong, the field of view is too narrow, the eye tracking is fussy in low light, the price is absurd. None of that matters. What matters is that the rectangle is gone. When you put the headset on and look up, there is no top of the page. When you look behind you, the interface does not stop.

The first thing that broke in my brain was hierarchy. On a screen, importance is signalled by size, weight, and position. The hero is up top. Secondary content lives in a sidebar. The footer is for the things you do not really want anyone to read. None of that survives in space. When the user can stand up and walk three feet to their left, "up top" stops meaning anything. Distance does the work that font weight used to.

The second thing that broke was the idea of a page. Pages have edges. Edges create composition. Composition is most of what visual design teaches you. In a spatial scene, the edge is the user's own head turning. You are no longer composing a still image. You are composing a behavior, an arc of attention, a relationship between a person and a room that they are physically inside of.

The third thing that broke was the assumption of urgency. Screens demand. They ping, they badge, they animate, they push. The whole grammar of the web is interruption masquerading as service. When you put a notification inside someone's living room, even a small one, it feels rude in a way that the same notification on a phone never did. Space punishes greed.

I spent more time deleting things from my app than adding them. The first build had a menu, a settings panel, a progress bar, three confirmation dialogs, and a tutorial. The final build has none of those. The user looks at a flower, and the flower opens. The user closes their eyes, and the room dims. Everything else turned out to be screen-thinking dressed up in 3D clothes.

The phrase "spatial computing" is doing a lot of work in 2026, and most of the work it is doing is marketing. The actual shift, the one that matters, is much smaller and much harder. It is the shift from designing surfaces to designing presence. Surfaces are what you put information on. Presence is what you build when there is no surface left to hide behind.

Apple will sell a cheaper headset next year, and the year after that the form factor will start to disappear into a pair of glasses. By 2030, the rectangle will look the way the command line looks now: still around, still useful for specialists, but no longer the default mental model for what a computer is.

The designers who will matter in that world are not the ones who can make the prettiest floating window. They are the ones who can sit in a room and ask, calmly, what does this person actually need from this space, and how little can I add before I am in the way.

The rectangle was a workaround. We just forgot.
