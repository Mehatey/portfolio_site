## 4. What the winning work actually does

Verbatim from `2.cube/3d/generated/cube-guy-direction-v3/award-benchmark.md`, which
remains the source of record for the Cube Guy V3 direction. Kept here because it is
the only written distillation of the award research and nothing was ever going to
find it at that path.

1. One governing metaphor. KOKUYO uses pop-up construction. Monolith makes touch
   rewrite reality. Symphony of Vines uses one cinematic journey. Effects never feel
   imported from another project.
2. State changes carry meaning. Scroll does not simply rotate a model. It changes the
   character's condition, the camera's role, and what the visitor understands.
3. Strong silhouette before shaders. Hero objects remain readable in every frame.
   Particles and distortion support the focal subject instead of covering it.
4. Authored camera language. Close-up, reveal, crossing, wide environment,
   transformation, return. Not one locked camera with random parallax.
5. Interaction has consequence. Pointer response exposes personality or changes the
   world. It is not ambient noise.
6. Restraint. One hero, one transition language, one accent effect at a time.
7. Performance is designed into the visual system. Compressed assets, progressive
   loading, controlled DPR, and lazy scene activation make heavy work feel smooth.

### The V2 rejection list

Written as a record of what was tried and thrown out. Do not re-propose these.

- Generic mannequin silhouette
- Scans selected by availability, not narrative need
- Foreground geometry obscuring the hero
- Panorama used as wallpaper instead of spatial composition
- Particle quantity mistaken for craft
- Scroll clips without causal storytelling
- Dissolve represented by unrelated white fragments
- Camera and character motion competing simultaneously

## 7. Joe Parker-Rees, product designer to design engineer

- [Work](https://www.joeparkerrees.com/#work)
- [Info](https://www.joeparkerrees.com/info)
- [Playground](https://www.joeparkerrees.com/playground)
- [AI reference](https://www.joeparkerrees.com/ai-reference)

Studied in full on 7 November 2026: home, Info, four main case studies, the
Playground index, all fourteen individual Playground pages, and the AI reference.
This is the closest career and portfolio reference in this file. Joe comes from
brand and visual design, now designs and builds products, uses AI in execution,
and presents technical experiments without letting them obscure the hiring story.

### Why it feels clear

1. **One continuous body of work.** Work and Playground are two levels of the
   same practice, not unrelated identities. Work holds the most complete stories.
   Playground holds smaller working things and technical write-ups. Every item can
   be opened, understood, and evaluated.
2. **The index makes a decision easy.** Each project gets a name, one plainspoken
   sentence, useful discipline labels, and a clear indication of whether a full
   case study exists. There is no second metadata taxonomy competing with it.
3. **The opening sentence earns attention.** Main cases use a short, memorable
   premise instead of a generic project summary. The sentence states the actual
   situation or tension, then the page immediately shows the product.
4. **Copy admits reality.** Unshipped pitches are labelled unshipped. Mock data,
   solo work, borrowed interaction ideas, limitations, client rejection, and weak
   first attempts are named directly. This makes the stronger claims believable.
5. **The final work appears before the process.** A visitor sees what exists, what
   it does, the result, the role, and the team before reading how it was made.
6. **Process is written as decisions.** The strongest sections follow a repeatable
   causal shape: what felt wrong, what the first version did, why it failed, what
   changed, and what was learned. Screens, code, and interactive demos are evidence,
   not decoration.
7. **Technical depth stays legible.** Code appears beside the interaction it
   explains. Performance choices are expressed as visible before and after changes,
   not as a list of tools.
8. **Every experiment has a reason to exist.** Playground entries begin with a
   curiosity, reference, irritation, or personal need. Even tiny experiments show
   the working version and one concrete lesson.
9. **Info answers placement questions.** It quickly explains background, current
   direction, working style, present status, and the kind of team he wants. It does
   not reproduce a resume.
10. **Navigation uses words.** Work, Info, Playground, and Contact are always
    understandable. Personality comes from the work and interactions, not from
    asking visitors to decode primary navigation.

### Page patterns worth adapting

**Work index**

- Use one sentence per project that combines the problem, product, and strongest
  proof. Do not stack category, marketing summary, metric, role, number, and award
  as separate competing lines.
- Make full case studies and smaller experiments visually related. Distinguish them
  with an honest type label or destination, not a completely different interface.
- Keep Sid's visual covers and atmospheric system. Borrow the clarity, not Joe's
  neutral card aesthetic.

**Case study opening**

- Lead with project name, one memorable premise, the final experience, then a
  compact facts group: outcome, role, team, duration. Only show facts that answer a
  recruiter question.
- Place outcome before process when evidence exists. If a project did not ship,
  state that plainly and explain what the project still proves.
- Let visitors see the first product tour before requiring the long case story.

**Case study body**

- Structure chapters around decisions, not deliverable categories such as
  “Research”, “Ideation”, and “Final UI” unless those labels carry real meaning.
- Pair each process claim with the artifact that proves it. Remove media that does
  not advance the story.
- Include failed iterations selectively. One good comparison builds more trust than
  a gallery of unexplained process images.
- Make live prototypes, award pages, publications, source code, and shipped links
  contextual actions near the relevant proof.

**Play**

- Treat Play as Sid's working lab for AI prototypes, creative technology, games,
  visual systems, and short technical experiments.
- Each item should answer three questions: what prompted it, what can I try, and
  what did Sid learn?
- Move personal photography and video to their own clearly named camera archive.
  They show taste, but should not interrupt the design-engineering sequence.
- Let strong Play entries open into short write-ups. Not every experiment needs the
  machinery of a full case study.

**About / Info**

- Start with direct placement: product and visual designer who builds, with a
  background in brand, UI, and experimental technology.
- Explain the transition into design engineering with concrete work, not a title
  claim alone.
- State what Sid is strongest at, how he works with teams, what he is learning, and
  what role he wants next. Keep limitations honest where useful.

### Copy rules to carry into Sid's site

- Write as Sid speaks. Specific, short, and willing to admit uncertainty.
- Prefer “I changed X because Y failed” over “I leveraged an iterative process.”
- Prefer the actual constraint over a grand project description.
- Say whether something shipped, was a pitch, used mock data, or was self-initiated.
- Keep metrics only when their source and meaning are defensible.
- Do not imitate Joe's jokes, sentences, page layouts, or visual system. The lesson
  is candour and causal storytelling, not voice cloning.

### Immediate application to this portfolio

1. Rewrite the Works index to one strong line per project and one destination cue.
2. Standardise case openings around premise, final work, outcome, role, team, and
   duration. Remove repeated labels and empty statistics.
3. Curate each long case into final result first, then three to five decision-led
   chapters. Archive unexplained galleries rather than showing everything.
4. Make Play the shared home for creative technology and small build experiments.
   Give the best entries short working write-ups.
5. Keep Photos as a separate camera archive.
6. Rewrite About around Sid's real transition from visual and brand design into
   product design and design engineering, plus the exact role he wants now.
7. Consider a factual machine-readable profile later, but only after the human path
   is concise. Joe's AI reference works because every claim points back to visible
   evidence.

### Do not copy

- Neutral rounded cards as the site's dominant visual language.
- His typography, colour palette, page chrome, labels, or jokes.
- Long technical explanation where Sid has no real artifact or learning to show.
- A large AI reference page before Works, Play, About, and Contact are resolved.

The portable principle: **show the thing, say what was real, explain the decision,
then let the visitor go deeper.**
